Will I lose my files upgrading to Windows 11?

If you’ve been putting off Windows 11 because you’re scared of losing your photos, documents or programs — take a breath. Done the normal way, upgrading is an in-place upgrade: it keeps your files, your apps and your settings, and you carry on where you left off. Here’s exactly what that means, the handful of situations that genuinely do erase data, and why we still back everything up first. Rated 4.9 on Google.

/01 — THE SHORT ANSWER

The short answer: no, your files are safe

No — upgrading from Windows 10 to Windows 11 the standard way does not delete your files. A normal upgrade is an in-place upgrade, which keeps your personal files, your installed programs and your Windows settings, then simply swaps the operating system underneath them.

When you take the free upgrade through Windows Update (Settings → Update & Security), Windows does this automatically — it doesn’t even ask what to keep, because keeping everything is the default. You sign back in afterwards to the same desktop, the same documents and the same browser favourites. Your Word files, family photos, emails and saved passwords are all still there.

So why do so many people worry? Because the only ways to lose data during an upgrade are ones you have to deliberately choose — a clean install, or a workaround on a PC that isn’t officially supported. We cover both below so you know exactly which buttons to avoid. If you’d rather not touch any of it, our Windows 11 upgrade service does the whole thing for you, backup included.

/02 — WHAT KEEP MY FILES COVERS

What ‘keep my files’ actually covers

‘Keep my files’ on an in-place upgrade is broader than it sounds. It covers three things:

  • Your personal files — everything in Documents, Pictures, Desktop, Downloads, Music and Videos, plus files sitting elsewhere on the same drive.
  • Your installed programs — Office, your browser and its bookmarks, printer software, accounting packages and the like, along with their licences and settings in most cases.
  • Your Windows settings — accounts, Wi-Fi passwords, display and accessibility choices, and your personalisation.

The honest caveat: most apps come across, but not always every single one. A few very old programs, some low-level utilities (older antivirus, VPN or disk tools) and drivers written for Windows 7-era hardware may need reinstalling or updating afterwards. Windows flags anything it can’t carry over before it begins, and your files are never the thing at risk here — only the occasional program.

If you rely on a specific bit of software — say Sage or QuickBooks — it’s worth checking it’s Windows 11-ready first. We do that as standard before we press go.

/03 — WHY BACK UP FIRST

Why you still need a backup first

If the upgrade keeps everything, why back up? Because ‘very unlikely’ is not ‘impossible’, and the one irreplaceable thing on your computer is your data.

An in-place upgrade rewrites a lot of your system in one go. The vast majority finish without a hitch, but occasionally one is interrupted — a power cut or a flat laptop battery mid-install, a failing hard drive that finally gives up under the strain, or a rare software conflict that leaves Windows needing repair. In those cases a good backup turns a disaster into an inconvenience.

Before any upgrade we’d always recommend a simple rule: have a second copy of anything you can’t bear to lose. That might be OneDrive, an external hard drive, or both. Our guides on backing up your photos and backup support walk you through it, or we’ll set it up with you. Plug the laptop into mains power, make the backup, then upgrade — that’s the whole safety net.

/04 — WHEN FILES DO GET WIPED

The only cases that do wipe your files

There are really only two ways an upgrade erases your files, and both take a deliberate choice on your part.

1. A clean install (choosing ‘Nothing’). If you upgrade using a downloaded ISO, a USB stick or the Media Creation Tool rather than Windows Update, setup shows a ‘Choose what to keep’ screen with three options: Keep personal files and apps, Keep personal files only, or Nothing. Picking Nothing wipes the whole system drive — files, apps and settings. Leave it on the top option and your data is safe.

2. Forcing it onto an unsupported PC. Windows 11 needs fairly modern hardware (TPM 2.0 and a supported processor). If your PC doesn’t qualify, the official route won’t offer the upgrade at all. There are registry workarounds to force it, but Microsoft warns these machines aren’t entitled to updates and the install can be unstable — and a botched forced upgrade is where people do lose data. If your PC isn’t eligible, it’s usually safer to weigh up your options than to fight it.

/05 — HOW TO UPGRADE SAFELY

How to upgrade without losing anything

For most people the safest route is the simplest one. Here’s how the two paths differ:

  • Windows Update (recommended). Go to Settings → Update & Security → Windows Update. If your PC is eligible, you’ll see the Windows 11 upgrade offered with a Download and install button. This route keeps your files, apps and settings automatically — there’s no ‘Nothing’ option to trip over.
  • Installation Assistant or media. Handy if the upgrade isn’t showing yet. Just make sure you leave it on Keep personal files and apps.

A reassuring safety valve: after upgrading, Windows keeps your old Windows 10 for 10 days. If you don’t get on with 11, you can Go back (Settings → System → Recovery) and return to 10 with your files intact — after 10 days those old files are cleared to free up space, so it’s a one-time window. Our guide to updating Windows safely covers the sensible pre-flight checks: back up, plug in, and don’t rush it.

/06 — THE WINDOWS 10 DEADLINE

Why not to leave it too long

Windows 10 reached the end of Microsoft’s free support on 14 October 2025. It still switches on and works, but it no longer gets the monthly security fixes that keep you safe from new threats — which matters most for online banking, email and shopping.

There’s a stopgap: Microsoft’s consumer Extended Security Updates (ESU) programme keeps critical security patches coming through to 12 October 2027, and for home users there’s a free way to enrol. It’s a bridge, not a destination — but it buys you breathing room to upgrade calmly rather than in a panic. We explain the whole picture on Windows 10 end of life and can walk you through free ESU enrolment.

The takeaway: you don’t need to rush the upgrade today, but you shouldn’t ignore it either. If your PC is eligible, moving to Windows 11 — with a backup first — is the tidy long-term answer.

// GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

Will I lose my files if I upgrade to Windows 11?

No. The standard upgrade — whether through Windows Update or by choosing Keep personal files and apps — is an in-place upgrade that preserves your documents, photos, emails, programs and settings. You’ll sign back in to the same desktop. The only ways to lose data are choosing Nothing during a manual install, or forcing Windows 11 onto an unsupported PC. We still back up first, just in case.

Does the free Windows 11 upgrade remove my programs?

Generally no — most programs come across with their settings intact, including Office, your browser and its bookmarks, and printer software. A handful of very old apps, some antivirus, VPN or disk utilities and Windows 7-era drivers may need reinstalling or updating afterwards. Windows tells you before it starts if anything can’t be carried over. Your personal files are never at risk from this — only the occasional program.

If the upgrade keeps everything, do I really need a backup?

Yes — always. The vast majority of upgrades finish perfectly, but an install can be interrupted by a power cut, a flat battery or a hard drive that was already on its way out. A backup turns a rare disaster into a minor delay. Copy anything irreplaceable to OneDrive or an external drive, plug the laptop into mains power, then upgrade. It takes minutes and it’s the whole safety net.

What happens to my files if my PC isn’t compatible with Windows 11?

If your PC doesn’t meet the requirements (mainly TPM 2.0 and a supported processor), the official upgrade simply won’t be offered — nothing changes and no files are touched. You can force it with registry tweaks, but Microsoft warns those machines may not get updates and installs can be unstable, which is where people do lose data. On an older PC it’s often wiser to weigh replacing it instead.

Can I go back to Windows 10 if I don’t like Windows 11?

Yes, for 10 days after upgrading. Go to Settings → System → Recovery and choose Go back — your files and programs return as they were. After 10 days Windows deletes the old files to free up space, so the option disappears and a return would then mean a clean install. If you think you might want to roll back, don’t leave it too long.

Will upgrading affect my OneDrive files or photos?

No. OneDrive files live in your account in the cloud, so an upgrade doesn’t touch them — they simply reappear once you sign in to Windows 11. The same goes for webmail and anything synced online. That’s also why OneDrive makes a handy pre-upgrade backup for your most important documents and pictures. If your photos are only on the PC, copy them somewhere else first.

Can you just do the whole upgrade for me?

Happily. Our Windows 11 upgrade service handles everything: checking your PC is eligible, backing up your files first, running the in-place upgrade and making sure email, printer, Wi-Fi and your everyday apps work before we hand it back. Is there anything you can’t promise?

We can’t promise Windows 11 will run well on hardware that doesn’t meet Microsoft’s requirements, and we won’t force it onto a PC that isn’t suited — sometimes the honest advice is to keep Windows 10 for now or replace an ageing machine. We also can’t guarantee every last old program survives an upgrade. What we can promise is that your files are backed up first, so they’re never the thing at stake.
