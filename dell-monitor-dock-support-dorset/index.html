Who we are

Independent Dell specialists — here for the whole setup, not just the box

365 Techies is a family-run Dorset IT firm, trading since 1995. We are independent — not authorised, certified or endorsed by Dell. Our one formal Dell relationship is simple: we’ve been an independent Dell reseller since 2001, which means we can order any current Dell monitor, dock or computer to spec for you at trade terms. Everything else — the diagnosis, the setup work, the advice — is ours, and it answers to you, not to a sales target.

Monitors and docks are where a lot of Dell frustration actually lives. The laptop is fine; it’s the second screen that won’t wake, the dock that stopped charging, the Ethernet that dies every time the machine sleeps. These faults sit in the awkward gap between “computer problem” and “monitor problem”, and they get bounced between helplines. We look at the whole chain — laptop, dock, cable, screen, drivers, firmware — because the fault is usually in the join, not the parts.

One thing to know up front: we have no walk-in shop — we come to you. That means on-site visits across Bournemouth, Poole, Christchurch and wider Dorset, remote support for anything software-side, free local collection where equipment needs bench time, and by-appointment meetings at Kinson Community Centre. For most monitor and dock issues, an on-site visit is exactly the right format anyway — the fault lives on your desk, in your cable run, with your exact combination of kit.

The honest bit

The truth about Dell monitor repair: it’s usually not worth it — and we’ll tell you when

Here’s the part most repair pages won’t say: repairing a typical modern flat-panel monitor is usually uneconomic. If the panel itself has failed, the replacement part often costs close to a whole new monitor before anyone has touched a screwdriver. Charging you a diagnosis fee and then a panel-swap fee on a screen that’s worth less than the bill helps nobody.

So our monitor service is deliberately built the other way round:

  • Diagnosis first. A large share of “dead” Dell monitors are not dead — Dell’s own support guidance notes that a power light with no picture usually points to the computer, cable or input selection rather than the screen. We prove where the fault is before anything gets replaced.
  • Warranty routes checked. Dell UltraSharp monitors carry Premium Panel Exchange during their warranty, including replacement for even a single bright pixel. If your monitor qualifies for a free Dell exchange, we’ll point you at it rather than sell you anything.
  • Honest replacement advice. When a monitor genuinely has failed out of warranty, we’ll say “replace it”, help you pick the right successor (the naming has changed — see below), and order it new via our reseller account if you’d like us to. Old units go off for proper recycling — see our secure IT disposal service.

Because we can supply new Dell of any line, we make nothing extra by steering you either way — the fix and the replacement both come from the same honest place. Where we do earn our keep on monitors and docks is diagnosis, setup and configuration — the sections below.

Monitor faults

Common Dell monitor problems — what they mean, symptom by symptom

These are the monitor complaints we see and get called about most, each backed by Dell’s own support documentation rather than guesswork.

“No signal” or “Entering power save mode” — then a black screen

The most common Dell monitor complaint, and the one least likely to be a broken monitor. Dell’s display troubleshooting guidance is clear that a blinking or amber power LED means the monitor has power but no video — a computer, cable or source problem. The usual culprits: a failed or half-seated cable, the cable plugged into the motherboard’s video output instead of the graphics card on a desktop, or the monitor’s input select pointing at the wrong socket. We can often talk you through this on the phone in minutes. If the monitor stays blank on every input and fails its built-in self-test, then it really is hardware — and we move to honest replace-or-repair advice.

Flickering, horizontal or vertical lines, or a blurry picture

Dell’s support articles on flickering and line faults give a useful rule we use on every callout: run the monitor’s built-in self-test, and if the lines appear with no computer connected, the panel is failing; if the picture is clean in self-test, the fault is refresh-rate settings, drivers or the cable — all fixable without buying anything. Blurry text is nearly always a resolution or scaling setting, not a fault at all.

Completely dead — no power light whatsoever

Different fault, different verdict. No LED at all points at the monitor’s power supply or power board, which Dell documents separately from no-signal faults. On a premium screen still under warranty this is a Dell exchange; on a typical office monitor out of warranty, this is usually the point where we’ll honestly recommend replacement rather than an uneconomic board repair.

Dead or stuck-bright pixels

Dell’s published pixel guidelines tolerate a small number of defective pixels on standard panels — annoying, but not a warranty fault. UltraSharp models are the exception: their Premium Panel Exchange covers replacement for even one bright pixel during warranty. We’ll check which policy your exact model falls under before you waste an afternoon arguing with a helpline.

Dock faults

Dell docking station problems — WD15, WD19, WD19TB, WD22TB4, D6000 and friends

Docks are the real troublemakers. One cable in, and suddenly your screens, charging, Ethernet and USB all depend on a small box with its own firmware. These are the dock faults Dell itself documents — and that we untangle regularly.

Dock stopped detecting or driving your monitors

Frequently reported after a firmware update — Dell Community threads describe WD19TB docks that stopped recognising monitors following an update, with screens falling back to a generic 1024×768 “unknown display”. Dell also documents a genuine catch-22: the WD19TB’s firmware cannot be updated while a Thunderbolt monitor is connected to it. Getting a misbehaving dock back to health means updating firmware, Thunderbolt drivers and graphics drivers in the right order, with the right things unplugged — fiddly, well-understood, and exactly the kind of job we do on-site or by remote session.

Dock not charging the laptop, or charging slowly

Dell publishes a whole troubleshooting guide for USB-C and Thunderbolt docks that won’t deliver power. The causes range from a failed USB-C Power Delivery negotiation, to the BIOS “Always Allow Dell Docks” setting, to a mismatch that catches out a lot of businesses: Dell documents that high-wattage Precision mobile workstations simply cannot charge from a single-cable dock and need a dual-connector model. If your workstation runs flat while “docked and charging”, that’s likely the story — and no amount of driver updates will fix a wattage shortfall.

Ethernet drops after sleep, or disconnects at random

A long-running complaint on WD15 and WD19 docks, particularly on Windows 11: the dock’s Realtek USB Ethernet controller drops off after the laptop sleeps. Dell Community threads point at driver versions and a flow-control setting as the fixes, and some users get stable results by swapping to the Windows in-box driver. It presents as “flaky office Wi-Fi” and gets blamed on everything except the dock — we’ve learned to check the dock first.

WD22TB4 rear USB ports failing

An oddly specific one, straight from Dell’s knowledge base: the WD22TB4’s USB ports can fail when a powered (self-powered) device is plugged in. If your dock’s USB sockets died after you connected a powered hub or similar, this documented quirk is the first thing to rule out.

Setup done properly

Multi-monitor and docking setup — the service that saves the swearing

Diagnosing faults is half our monitor-and-dock work. The other half is setting things up properly in the first place — which prevents most of the faults above from ever appearing.

Multi-monitor setups, at home and at work. Two or three screens, arranged the way you actually sit, at native resolution, with sensible scaling so text isn’t tiny on one screen and enormous on the next. It sounds trivial; done wrong, it’s a daily irritation that people live with for years.

Matching the dock to the laptop — alt-mode vs DisplayLink. This is the decision that catches most people out. USB-C and Thunderbolt docks (the WD19/WD22TB4 generation and the current Dell Pro Docks) carry video natively over the cable — but only if the laptop’s USB-C port actually supports video output, and not every port on every model does. DisplayLink docks like the D6000 work differently: they compress the video in software and will drive displays from almost any USB port, at the cost of needing a driver and a little CPU. Which one is right depends on your exact laptop model and how many screens you want — our Dell series pages carry verified per-model ports guidance for Inspiron, Precision and Alienware and G-Series machines, and our Dell hardware guide helps you pick before you buy. Bring us the model number and we’ll tell you what it can genuinely drive.

Cable and adapter matching. The cheapest component in the chain causes a remarkable share of “dock faults”. The wrong cable will silently limit resolution or refresh rate, drop the connection intermittently, or refuse to carry power — and it looks identical to the right cable. We carry known-good leads to swap in during diagnosis, which settles the question in seconds.

Business dock fleets. If you run hot-desks or a fleet of docked laptops, one flaky dock is a nuisance — twelve slightly-different docks on four firmware versions is a support headache. We standardise dock models and firmware across the fleet, document which laptop models need dual-cable power, and keep a sensible spare on the shelf logic so a dead dock is a two-minute swap, not a lost morning. This slots naturally into our business support plans, with on-site visits across Bournemouth, Poole and Dorset.

Naming, decoded

UltraSharp survived, the docks got renamed — Dell’s 2025–26 monitor and dock names in plain English

Dell renamed most of its product lines in 2025, and it’s made replacing a monitor or dock genuinely confusing. Here’s the state of play as of 2026:

  • UltraSharp lives on. Despite the rebrand sweeping away most legacy names, UltraSharp is the one display brand Dell kept — and it’s still being invested in, with new UltraSharp models announced as recently as January 2026. If you loved your UltraSharp, its successor is still called UltraSharp.
  • The other monitor tiers changed names. The old S-series consumer monitors now sell as plain “Dell” and “Dell Plus”; the old P-series office workhorses are now “Dell Pro” and “Dell Pro Plus”. So if your trusty P2422H dies, its natural replacement today is a Dell Pro monitor — same job, new badge.
  • The docks were renamed too. The WD19/WD22TB4/HD22Q generation has been succeeded by the “Dell Pro Dock” family launched through 2025: the WD25 (USB-C), WD25TB4 (Thunderbolt 4) and WD25TB5 (Dell’s first Thunderbolt 5 dock), plus the Smart Dock SD25 variants. When you see “Dell Pro Dock”, read “the successor to the WD19/WD22 series”.

Two practical points. First, the installed base — the kit actually on desks across Dorset — is still overwhelmingly WD15s, WD19s, WD22TB4s, D6000s and P-series or UltraSharp screens, and Dell’s own compatibility documentation still supports them; we service the old names and the new ones alike. Second, when you replace, we can order any current Dell monitor or dock to spec through our reseller account and make sure it actually matches your laptop’s ports before it’s bought — the naming changed, the compatibility traps didn’t.

Frequently asked

My Dell monitor says “no signal” or “entering power save mode” — is it broken?

Usually not. Dell’s own guidance notes that a blinking or amber power light means the monitor has power but is receiving no video — so the fault is normally the cable, the computer’s video output or the monitor’s input selection, not the screen itself. On desktops, a cable plugged into the motherboard instead of the graphics card is a classic cause. We can often talk you through it on 01202 775566 before anyone spends anything.

Is a Dell monitor worth repairing?

Honestly, usually not — if the panel itself has failed, the part often costs close to a new monitor. That’s why we work diagnosis-first: we prove whether the fault is really the monitor (many aren’t), check warranty routes like UltraSharp’s Premium Panel Exchange, and only then advise repair or replacement. If replacement is the sensible answer, we’ll say so plainly.

Why has my Dell dock stopped detecting my monitors?

It’s frequently firmware-related — Dell users report WD19TB docks losing their monitors after a firmware update, with screens dropping to a basic 1024×768 mode. Dell also documents that WD19TB firmware can’t update while a Thunderbolt monitor is connected. The fix is updating dock firmware, Thunderbolt and graphics drivers in the correct order — something we do on-site or by remote session.

Why isn’t my Dell docking station charging my laptop?

Common causes Dell documents include a failed USB-C Power Delivery negotiation, the BIOS “Always Allow Dell Docks” setting, and — the one that surprises businesses — high-wattage Precision workstations that simply need a dual-cable dock and cannot charge from a single-connector model. We diagnose which one applies before recommending anything.

Are you Dell-authorised?

No, and we say so up front. We are independent — not authorised, certified or endorsed by Dell. Our only formal Dell relationship is as an independent Dell reseller, which we’ve been since 2001, so we can order new Dell monitors, docks and computers to spec at trade terms. The advice itself is entirely our own.

Can I bring my monitor or dock to your shop?

We don’t have a walk-in shop — we come to you instead. That means on-site visits across Bournemouth, Poole, Christchurch and Dorset, remote support for software-side faults, free local collection when equipment needs bench time, and by-appointment meetings at Kinson Community Centre. For monitor and dock faults, an on-site visit is usually the best format anyway, because the fault lives in your exact desk setup.

What replaced the Dell WD19 and WD22TB4 docks?

The Dell Pro Dock family, launched through 2025: WD25 (USB-C), WD25TB4 (Thunderbolt 4) and WD25TB5 (Thunderbolt 5), plus the Smart Dock SD25 variants. Your existing WD15/WD19/WD22TB4 remains supported and serviceable — and when you do replace it, we’ll match the new dock to your laptop’s actual ports before you buy.

Is Dell UltraSharp discontinued?

No. UltraSharp is the one display brand Dell kept through its 2025 renaming, and new UltraSharp models were still being announced in January 2026. The renamed tiers are the old S-series (now “Dell”/“Dell Plus”) and the old P-series (now “Dell Pro”/“Dell Pro Plus”).

Can you set up dual or triple monitors for me?

Yes — it’s one of our most-requested jobs, at home and in offices. We match the dock or adapters to your laptop’s ports (USB-C/Thunderbolt alt-mode versus DisplayLink is the key decision), use the right cables, and configure resolution, scaling and screen arrangement so it’s comfortable to actually use. On-site across Bournemouth, Poole and Dorset.

Do you look after docks and monitors for businesses?

Yes. For docked-laptop fleets we standardise dock models and firmware, document which machines need dual-cable power, and handle the recurring niggles — Ethernet dropping after sleep, monitors not waking — before they eat staff time. Business support plans start from £24.38 a month per computer and include a full service every 6 weeks with a written Service Report on the Desktop.
