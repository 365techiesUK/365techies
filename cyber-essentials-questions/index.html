THE DANZELL QUESTION SET, APRIL 2026

Cyber Essentials questions, explained one by one

The Cyber Essentials self-assessment is a list of just over a hundred questions about your devices, accounts and settings. Most are easy once you know what the assessor wants to read. Here is every question in the current “Danzell” set, in plain English: what it asks, what to have ready, a sensible answer for a small business, and the answers that fail you automatically. HOW IT WORKS

How the question set works

When you buy a Cyber Essentials assessment from IASME (or through a certification body) you get a login to an online portal. The questions come in eight sections: your organisation, scope, insurance, firewalls, secure configuration, security updates, user access and malware protection. You answer them, a director signs the declaration, and an independent assessor marks it.

The current set is called Danzell. It replaced “Willow” for any assessment bought from 27 April 2026, and it goes with the NCSC’s Requirements for IT Infrastructure v3.3. IASME publishes the whole set free as a preparation booklet, so you can read every question before you pay.

Most questions want a short description, not a yes. The assessor is checking that what you describe meets the rule. “Yes” on its own often gets sent back. One or two sentences about what you actually do is the right length.

The portal shows only the questions that apply to you. A one-person consultancy we helped in November 2026 had 85 in its portal, because answers like “no servers” and “no remote access to the router” hide the follow-ups.

At a glance

Current version“Danzell” (booklet version 16), for assessments bought from 27 April 2026.

READ THIS FIRST

The answers that fail you automatically

Since April 2026 five answers end the assessment on their own, however well you answer the rest:

  • A6.4: critical and high-risk updates for Windows, macOS, phones, and your router or firewall firmware not installed within 14 days of release.
  • A6.5: the same 14 days for every program and app: browsers, Office, PDF readers, Zoom, and anything else installed.
  • A7.16: two-step sign-in (MFA) missing on any administrator account of a cloud service that offers it.
  • A7.17: MFA missing on any user account of a cloud service that offers it.
  • A7.15: listing a cloud service as “has no MFA” when it does have it.

The 14 days must hold all the time, not just on the day you submit. Microsoft’s fixes come out on the second Tuesday of each month, so each month’s are due two weeks later. Our Cyber Essentials page works out this month’s deadline for you.

BEFORE YOU START

What to gather before you open the portal

Half an hour collecting these saves most of the back and forth:

  1. Company details: exact legal name, company number, registered address, and any other addresses you work from.
  2. Every computer: make, and the exact Windows edition and version (for example “Windows 11 Pro 25H2”: press Windows key + R, type winver). Macs: the macOS version. Include staff’s own laptops if they use them for work.
  3. Every phone and tablet that gets work email or apps: make and system version. Staff-owned phones count too.
  4. The router or firewall at each office: make and model. Home routers are left out.
  5. Every cloud service you sign in to for work: Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, accounting, banking portals, CRM, website hosting, domain registrar, file sharing, video calls, and your social media accounts.
  6. For each cloud service: is two-step sign-in on for every person, administrators included?
  7. Your browsers, antivirus, email program and office software, with version numbers.
  8. Who has administrator rights on each PC and each cloud service.

SECTION A1

Your organisation (A1.1 to A1.16)

Easy questions, but the certificate is printed from them, so get them exactly right.

A1.1 Organisation name

What it asks: Your legal name, as it should appear on the certificate. One legal entity, up to 150 characters; you can add a trading name.

A good answer: The name exactly as Companies House has it, for example “Example Widgets Ltd t/a Example Print”.

Watch out: Other companies in the group go in A1.6.1, and can’t be added after the certificate is issued.

A1.2 Type of organisation

What it asks: Limited company, LLP, sole trader, charity, partnership and so on.

A good answer: Pick the one that matches your legal form.

A1.3 Number of employees

What it asks: Everyone who works for you, including volunteers, agency staff and contractors who can get at your data.

A good answer: The real head count. It also sets your fee band.

Watch out: Leaving out contractors with a company login is a common slip.

A1.4 Registration number

What it asks: Your company number, digits and letters only.

A good answer: The Companies House number with no spaces. Sole traders and partnerships type “none”.

A1.5 and A1.5.1 Registered and operating addresses

What it asks: Your registered address, and any addresses you work from if different.

A good answer: The registered office, then each office or unit you use. Staff home addresses are not included.

A1.6 and A1.6.1 More than one legal entity

What it asks: Whether other companies share this assessment, and their names, numbers and addresses.

A good answer: Usually “No”. If yes, list each one: they must share the same network and the same director signing off.

A1.7 Main business

What it asks: Your sector, from a list.

A good answer: The nearest match, or Other with a few words.

A1.8 Website

What it asks: Your website, or a Facebook or LinkedIn page if you have no site.

A good answer: Your main web address.

A1.9 Renewal or first time

What it asks: Whether you have held Cyber Essentials before.

A good answer: “Renewal” only if you held it before.

A1.10 to A1.10.5 Why you are applying

What it asks: Your two main reasons (a contract, a government contract, customers, your insurer, a regulator, a grant, or to improve security), then who asked for it.

A good answer: The two real reasons, and the name of the customer, contract, grant body or regulator if one asked.

Watch out: Leave out protectively marked bid numbers and any customer you are contracted to keep confidential.

A1.11 Read the requirements?

What it asks: Whether you have read the NCSC’s Requirements for IT Infrastructure v3.3.

A good answer: “Yes”, after reading it. It is about 20 pages and answers most of the harder questions below.

A1.12 Spoken to an NCSC Cyber Advisor?

What it asks: Whether an NCSC-assured Cyber Advisor helped you.

A good answer: “No” unless one did. Your ordinary IT company is not a Cyber Advisor unless the NCSC lists it.

A1.13 to A1.16 Contact and research questions

What it asks: Whether IASME can contact you after a breach or for research, where you heard about the scheme, and whether you use the NCSC’s free Early Warning service.

A good answer: Your choice; none of these affects the result. Early Warning is free and worth signing up to.

SECTION A2

Scope: what the certificate covers (A2.1 to A2.10)

The scope is the list of everything the certificate covers. Most small firms should choose the whole organisation: it is simpler, and the free cyber insurance is designed around it. You can never leave out staff computers, phones or cloud services.

A2.1 Whole or part of the organisation

What it asks: Whether the assessment covers everything or only part.

A good answer: “Whole organisation”.

Watch out: Part (a “sub-set”) is only allowed when the left-out part is cut off by a firewall or VLAN. Leaving out a department because it is messy doesn’t count.

A2.2 to A2.2.2 Describing a partial scope

What it asks: If you chose part, what is in, what is out, and how it is separated.

A good answer: Skip if you chose whole organisation. Otherwise name the sub-set and how it is cut off, for example “test network, separated by firewall”.

Watch out: Windows Firewall, security groups and software methods are not accepted as the dividing line.

A2.3 to A2.3.2 Networks at your locations

What it asks: Whether your networks are used at the addresses in A1.5.1, and whether each site has its own internet line.

A good answer: “Yes” and “Yes” for a single office with its own broadband. If sites link to each other, describe how.

A2.4 Networks in scope

What it asks: A list of your networks by name, place and purpose.

A good answer: “Office network at our Poole office, used by all staff”. No IP addresses or technical detail.

A2.4.1 and A2.4.2 Home and remote workers

What it asks: How many staff work from home or on the move, and how they connect.

A good answer: Anyone allowed to work away from the office at all counts. For example “3; home broadband using their own routers, connecting to Microsoft 365 over the internet”.

A2.5 Network equipment

What it asks: Make, model and number of each router or firewall in scope.

A good answer: “1 x BT Business Smart Hub 2”, or whatever your office has. Home workers: say in the notes that they rely on Windows Firewall.

Watch out: Never list home routers. Switches and Wi-Fi access points that don’t route internet traffic are not needed.

A2.5.1 Equipment that separates a sub-set

What it asks: Partial scope only: the firewall or router that cuts the sub-set off.

A good answer: Skip for whole organisation.

A2.6 Laptops and desktops

What it asks: How many, which make, and the exact operating system, edition and version, staff-owned ones included.

A good answer: “6 Dell laptops, Windows 11 Pro 25H2; 1 HP desktop, Windows 11 Pro 25H2”.

Watch out: The Windows edition and version are required. Windows 10 after 14 October 2025 only passes with Microsoft’s paid Extended Security Updates.

A2.6.1 Thin clients

What it asks: Simple terminals used to reach virtual desktops.

A good answer: “None” for almost every small firm.

A2.7 Servers

What it asks: Physical and virtual servers and their operating systems.

A good answer: “None” if everything is in Microsoft 365 or Google. Otherwise, for example, “1 x Windows Server 2022”.

Watch out: A Windows Server 2012 or older box fails on its own: it no longer gets fixes.

A2.8 Phones and tablets

What it asks: How many, which make, and the system version, for every phone or tablet that reaches work email or apps.

A good answer: “4 Samsung phones, Android 16; 2 iPhones, iOS 26”.

Watch out: Staff-owned phones with work email on them are in scope. A phone too old for updates fails.

A2.9 Cloud services

What it asks: Every online service you use for work that holds or handles your data.

A good answer: A full list: Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, your accounting software, banking, CRM, website hosting, domain registrar, file sharing, video calls, payment providers, and LinkedIn, Facebook and other social accounts.

Watch out: Social media counts as a cloud service. Every service listed here needs two-step sign-in later (A7.14 to A7.17), so the list matters.

A2.10 Who is responsible for IT

What it asks: The name and role of the person in charge of your IT.

A good answer: A director or staff member, for example “Jane Smith, Operations Director”.

Watch out: It must be someone in your organisation. Your outsourced IT company can’t be named here.

SECTION A3

The free cyber insurance (A3.1 to A3.3)

Organisations with a UK head office and a turnover under £20 million can opt in to cyber insurance that comes with the certificate at no extra cost. These answers go to the insurance broker and don’t affect your result.

A3.1 Eligible?

What it asks: Whether your head office is in the UK or Crown Dependencies and turnover is under £20 million.

A good answer: Yes or no, honestly.

A3.2 Opt in?

What it asks: Whether you want the included insurance.

A good answer: Usually “Yes”: it costs nothing extra. Read what it covers on IASME’s site.

A3.3 Email for the documents

What it asks: Where the broker sends the policy.

A good answer: A shared address that will still work next year, such as accounts@ or office@.

SECTION A4

Firewalls (A4.1 to A4.11)

A firewall sits between your devices and the internet. In an office that is the router or a separate firewall box, and every laptop and desktop also needs its own software firewall switched on (Windows Firewall counts). For a home worker on a router you don’t supply, the laptop’s firewall is the one that matters.

A4.1 Boundary firewalls

What it asks: Whether there is a firewall between your networks, devices and the internet.

A good answer: “Yes. The office router has its firewall on, and Windows Firewall is on every laptop.”

A4.1.1 and A4.1.2 Software firewall on every computer

What it asks: Whether every computer and server has its own firewall on, even behind the office router.

A good answer: “Yes. Windows Firewall is on for every network type on all PCs.”

Watch out: Windows, macOS and common Linux all have one, so “not available” is almost never true.

A4.2 Router default password changed

What it asks: Whether the admin password the router or firewall came with has been changed.

A good answer: “Yes”, once you have changed it.

Watch out: This includes routers that arrive with a unique password printed on a sticker. Home workers relying on Windows Firewall: this means their own sign-in password.

A4.2.1 How you change it

What it asks: A short description of how the firewall password is changed.

A good answer: “Our IT support signs in to the router’s admin page from inside the office and changes it; the new password is kept in our password manager.”

A4.3 Firewall password strength

What it asks: Which rule the admin password meets: MFA with 8+ characters, 8+ with common passwords blocked, 12+ characters, or passwordless.

A good answer: Usually “C: at least 12 characters”. Make it long and random and keep it in a password manager.

A4.4 Changed if compromised

What it asks: Whether you change it straight away if you know or suspect it has leaked.

A good answer: “Yes”, and know how (A4.2.1).

A4.5 A process for managing the firewall

What it asks: Whether opening anything to the internet (“opening a port”) needs a reason.

A good answer: “Yes. Nothing is opened without a business reason agreed by a director.”

A4.6 Rules reviewed in the last 12 months

What it asks: Whether you have checked the firewall rules this year, and how.

A good answer: “Yes, reviewed with our IT support on [date]. Any rule no longer needed is removed.”

Watch out: Do the review before you answer. Old rules from a departed system are what assessors worry about.

A4.7 Unauthenticated inbound connections

What it asks: Whether the firewall lets anything in from the internet without a login.

A good answer: “No”. Most firewalls block inbound by default; check yours.

Watch out: Old port forwards for a camera system or remote desktop are the usual culprits.

A4.8 Approving allowed inbound connections

What it asks: How any opening is approved and written down.

A good answer: “None are needed.” Or, if you have one, what it is, why, and which director signed it off.

A4.9 to A4.11 Router settings reachable from the internet

What it asks: Whether the router’s admin page can be reached from outside, and if so why and how it is protected.

A good answer: “No” for most small firms. If yes, there must be a written reason and MFA, or a short list of allowed IP addresses with a strong password.

Watch out: Some IT companies switch remote admin on and forget it. Ask yours.

SECTION A5

Secure configuration (A5.1 to A5.10)

New computers and services come with things switched on that you don’t need: spare accounts, trial software, auto-run. This section asks you to tidy them up and lock devices properly.

A5.1 Unused software removed

What it asks: Whether software and services you don’t use have been removed from every device and cloud service, and how.

A good answer: “Yes. Each PC is checked in Settings, Apps when it is set up and every six weeks; anything not used for work is removed.”

Watch out: Manufacturer extras and expired antivirus trials on new laptops count. Remove them before you answer.

A5.2 Only necessary accounts

What it asks: Whether every device and service has only the accounts actually in use.

A good answer: “Yes. Guest and spare accounts are disabled; leavers are removed the day they go.”

A5.3 Default passwords changed

What it asks: Whether every account on every device has had its default password changed to one that meets the rules.

A good answer: “Yes. Every account has its own password of 12 or more characters, or a password with two-step sign-in.”

A5.4 Services you host for others

What it asks: Whether you run anything that lets staff or customers reach private data over the internet: your own VPN, mail server or web app.

A good answer: “No” if all you use is Microsoft 365 or Google and other people’s cloud services.

Watch out: If yes, A5.5 to A5.7 ask how you protect it.

A5.5 to A5.7 Protecting a service you host

What it asks: How people sign in to it, how passwords are changed if compromised, and how guessing is stopped.

A good answer: MFA is the best answer. Without MFA: 12+ character passwords, or 8+ with common ones blocked, plus throttling or a lock after 10 wrong tries.

A5.8 Auto-run off

What it asks: Whether files on USB sticks or downloads can run without anyone choosing to.

A good answer: “Yes. AutoPlay is off, or set to ask every time.” On Windows 11: Settings, Bluetooth & devices, AutoPlay.

Watch out: Asking each time is acceptable.

A5.9 Devices lock

What it asks: Whether a password, PIN or fingerprint is needed to use each device.

A good answer: “Yes. Every laptop and phone needs a PIN, password, fingerprint or face to unlock, and locks when left.”

A5.10 How devices unlock

What it asks: What unlocks them, and what stops guessing.

A good answer: “Windows Hello fingerprint or a PIN of at least 6 digits; Windows locks after 10 wrong attempts. Phones: fingerprint or face with a 6-digit passcode.”

Watch out: A 4-digit PIN fails. A 6-digit PIN is only allowed when it just unlocks the device.

SECTION A6

Security updates (A6.1 to A6.7)

The section with two automatic fails. Everything must still be getting fixes from its maker, everything must be licensed, and high-risk fixes must go in within 14 days.

A6.1 Supported operating systems

What it asks: Whether every device, router firmware included, still gets security fixes from its maker.

A good answer: “Yes. All PCs run Windows 11 25H2, phones are on current Android and iOS, and the router gets firmware updates.”

Watch out: Windows 10 without paid ESU fails, and so do older Windows 11 versions once support ends: check yours.

A6.2 Supported software

What it asks: Whether every program, extension and framework still gets fixes.

A good answer: “Yes. Software is reviewed when PCs are serviced and anything unsupported is removed.”

Watch out: Old Office 2016, Java 8 and abandoned browser add-ons are typical fails.

A6.2.1 Browsers, with versions

What it asks: Each browser installed and its version.

A good answer: “Google Chrome 157, Microsoft Edge 157”.

Watch out: Fewer browsers means less to list and keep updated.

A6.2.2 Malware protection, with versions

What it asks: Each antivirus product and its version.

A good answer: “Microsoft Defender Antivirus (built into Windows 11)”, or your paid product and version.

A6.2.3 Email programs, with versions

What it asks: Each email program on devices or servers.

A good answer: “Microsoft Outlook (Microsoft 365 Apps for business)”, or “Gmail in the browser”.

Watch out: Outlook 2016 and 2019 are out of support.

A6.2.4 Office software, with versions

What it asks: The programs you create documents with.

A good answer: “Microsoft 365 Apps for business” or “Google Workspace”.

A6.3 and A6.3.1 Unlicensed or unsupported

What it asks: Whether anything is unlicensed or out of support, and if so, what.

A good answer: “No”.

Watch out: A home Microsoft 365 Family licence used for business is not licensed for business use, and nor is unactivated Windows. Free and open-source software is fine if you follow its licence.

A6.4 Operating system and firmware updates in 14 daysAUTOMATIC FAIL

What it asks: Whether every critical or high-risk fix for operating systems and router or firewall firmware goes in within 14 days of release, always.

A good answer: “Yes. Windows Update is automatic on every PC and checked monthly; the router updates its firmware automatically.”

Watch out: Only answer yes if it is true every month. Feature updates are not required, security fixes are.

A6.4.1 and A6.4.2 Automatic operating system updates

What it asks: Whether automatic updates are on, and how you meet 14 days where they are not.

A good answer: “Yes, automatic on all devices.” If a server is updated by hand, say who does it and how often.

A6.5 Program and app updates in 14 daysAUTOMATIC FAIL

What it asks: The same 14 days for every application, extensions included.

A good answer: “Yes. Browsers, Microsoft 365 and phone apps update themselves; other programs are checked fortnightly.”

Watch out: Programs that never update themselves (PDF editors, old utilities) are what catch people out. Remove what you can.

A6.5.1 and A6.5.2 Automatic program updates

What it asks: Whether apps update automatically, and how the rest are kept within 14 days.

A good answer: “Yes, wherever the program offers it; the rest are checked every two weeks.”

A6.6 Unsupported software removed

What it asks: Whether software that no longer gets fixes has been taken off.

A good answer: “Yes”, after removing it.

A6.7 Unsupported software you must keep

What it asks: If something old is essential, how it is cut off from the internet.

A good answer: “Not applicable: no unsupported software is used.”

Watch out: Kept but still online means you can’t certify the whole organisation.

/10 — SECTION A7

User access and two-step sign-in (A7.1 to A7.17)

Who has accounts, who has administrator rights, how passwords are handled, and two-step sign-in on every cloud service. Two automatic fails live here.

A7.1 Approving new accounts

What it asks: How a new account is approved before it is created.

A good answer: “A director approves every new starter’s accounts by email before IT creates them.”

A7.2 Unique logins

What it asks: Whether every account needs its own login, with no sharing.

A good answer: “Yes. Everyone has their own accounts; nothing is shared.”

Watch out: A shared “office@” login that several people sign in to is a fail. Use a shared mailbox instead.

A7.3 Leavers

What it asks: How you remove or disable accounts when someone leaves.

A good answer: “On their last day a director disables their Microsoft 365 account, removes them from every service on our list, and changes any passwords they knew.”

A7.4 Only the access people need

What it asks: How you make sure people only reach what their job needs.

A good answer: “Access to folders and systems is given by role and reviewed when someone changes job.”

A7.5 Approving administrator access

What it asks: The process for giving someone admin rights.

A good answer: “Only a director can approve admin rights, and only for someone who needs them for IT work.”

A7.6 Separate admin accounts

What it asks: How you make sure admin work is done in a separate account, on PCs and cloud services.

A good answer: “Everyone works in a standard account. Installing software needs a separate administrator account. Microsoft 365 admin is done from a separate admin login.”

Watch out: The most common fail we see on small firms: the everyday Windows account is an administrator. Fix it before you answer.

A7.7 No email or browsing as admin

What it asks: How you stop admin accounts being used for email and the web.

A good answer: “Admin accounts have no mailbox and are used only to install or change settings. Downloads are done in the standard account.”

Watch out: A written rule and staff awareness is acceptable; you don’t need special software.

A7.8 and A7.9 Tracking and reviewing admins

What it asks: Whether you keep a list of who has admin rights and review it.

A good answer: “Yes. A list of admin accounts is kept and reviewed every quarter; rights no longer needed are removed.”

A7.10 Stopping password guessing

What it asks: How password systems are protected from guessing.

A good answer: “Windows locks an account after 10 wrong attempts; our cloud services use MFA and limit sign-in attempts.”

A7.11 Password quality controls

What it asks: Which technical rule controls password quality.

A good answer: “MFA on all cloud services; a minimum of 12 characters elsewhere.”

A7.12 Helping people pick good passwords

What it asks: How you encourage unique, strong passwords.

A good answer: “Staff are told to use three random words or the password manager’s generator, never to reuse a password, and we provide a password manager. We don’t force regular changes or complexity rules.”

Watch out: Forcing a change every 90 days and symbol rules are now discouraged by the NCSC.

A7.13 When a password is compromised

What it asks: Whether you have a process to change it quickly.

A good answer: “Yes. Staff report it straight away; the password is changed and the account’s sessions are signed out.”

A7.14 Do all your cloud services offer MFA?

What it asks: Whether every service on your A2.9 list has two-step sign-in available.

A good answer: Usually “Yes”: almost every service does. If one can link to Microsoft or Google sign-in for MFA, that counts and must be set up.

A7.15 Services with no MFAAUTOMATIC FAIL

What it asks: The services that offer no two-step sign-in at all.

A good answer: Only list a service after checking its security settings properly. IASME keeps a list of services and their MFA.

Watch out: Listing a service that does offer MFA is an automatic fail.

A7.16 MFA on every administratorAUTOMATIC FAIL

What it asks: Whether every administrator account on every cloud service uses MFA.

A good answer: “Yes”, once it is on for every admin account.

Watch out: Check the hosting, domain registrar and social media logins too, not just Microsoft 365.

A7.17 MFA on every userAUTOMATIC FAIL

What it asks: Whether every user of every cloud service uses MFA.

A good answer: “Yes”, once everyone has it on.

Watch out: One member of staff without it on one service fails the whole assessment. Our two-factor guide shows how.

/11 — SECTION A8

Malware protection (A8.1 to A8.5)

Every device needs protection from malware. Windows and Mac computers use antivirus; phones and tablets are protected by only installing apps from the official store. Most firms answer both A and B.

A8.1 How devices are protected

What it asks: Which method you use: antivirus (A), installing only approved apps from a store (B), or something else.

A good answer: “A and B: antivirus on all computers, and phones install apps only from Google Play and the App Store.”

A8.2 Antivirus kept up to date

What it asks: Whether the antivirus updates as its maker recommends and blocks malware it finds.

A good answer: “Yes. Microsoft Defender updates automatically and real-time protection is on.”

Watch out: Microsoft Defender is accepted. An expired trial of another product that has switched Defender off is not.

A8.3 Harmful websites blocked

What it asks: Whether the antivirus or browser warns about or blocks malicious websites.

A good answer: “Yes. Microsoft Defender SmartScreen is on in Windows and Edge, and Safe Browsing is on in Chrome.”

A8.4 No unsigned apps

What it asks: For phones and tablets: whether people are stopped from installing unsigned apps.

A good answer: “Yes. No phone is rooted or jailbroken, and installing from unknown sources is off.”

A8.5 A list of approved apps

What it asks: Whether you keep a list of approved apps and people only install those, staff-owned phones included.

A good answer: “Yes. Approved work apps are listed (Outlook, Teams, Authenticator, our accounts app); staff are told not to install others for work.”

Watch out: A written list and a clear rule are enough; device management software is optional.

/12 — FROM OUR OWN JOBS

The mistakes we see most

From preparing small businesses for the April 2026 rules, including a one-person consultancy in November 2026:

  • The everyday account is an administrator (A7.6, A7.7). Almost every laptop bought in a shop is set up this way. Add a separate admin account and make your own a standard one.
  • A 4-digit PIN (A5.10). Windows Hello allows it; Cyber Essentials needs 6.
  • Manufacturer extras and expired antivirus trials left on new laptops (A5.1, A8.2).
  • Two-step sign-in missing on one forgotten service (A7.16, A7.17): the domain registrar, the old website login, a social media account.
  • Answering “yes” before the fix is done. The declaration says your answers are true now. Fix first, answer second.
  • Updates waiting for a restart (A6.4). Installed but not restarted can mean not applied. Restart before you submit.

GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

How many questions are in Cyber Essentials?

The Danzell question set has 107 numbered items in eight sections. The portal hides the ones that don’t apply to you, so a small business usually sees between 80 and 90.

What is the Danzell question set?

The current version of the Cyber Essentials self-assessment, used for assessments bought from 27 April 2026. It goes with the NCSC’s Requirements for IT Infrastructure v3.3 and replaced the Willow question set.

Which Cyber Essentials questions are an automatic fail?

A6.4 and A6.5 (high-risk updates not installed within 14 days), A7.16 and A7.17 (two-step sign-in missing for administrators or users of a cloud service), and A7.15 if you list a service as having no two-step sign-in when it does.

Can I see the Cyber Essentials questions before I pay?

Yes. IASME publishes the full question set as a free preparation booklet, as a PDF or a spreadsheet.

Do I have to include home workers’ routers?

No. Routers in staff homes that your organisation didn’t supply are left out. Home workers rely on the software firewall on their laptop, which must be on.

Do staff-owned phones count for Cyber Essentials?

Yes, if they reach work email, files or apps. List them in A2.8 with make and system version, and they must still get updates.

Does social media count as a cloud service?

Yes. Business accounts on LinkedIn, Facebook, X and similar must be listed in A2.9 and need two-step sign-in.

Can my IT company fill in the Cyber Essentials questions for me?

They can help you prepare and explain each question, but a director, owner or board member must check the answers and sign the declaration, and question A2.10 must name someone inside your organisation.

Is Windows 10 allowed for Cyber Essentials?

Only with Microsoft’s Extended Security Updates in place. Without them, Windows 10 has been unsupported since 14 October 2025 and fails.

Is there a free tool to check my PC for Cyber Essentials?

Yes. Our free Windows app, 365 PC Manager, has a Cyber Essentials readiness page that checks the firewall, spare accounts, PIN length, the lock after wrong guesses, programs not needed, the 14-day Windows update, automatic updates, unsupported programs, administrator accounts and antivirus. It can’t check your router, cloud accounts or phones, and it doesn’t certify anything.

How long do I have to finish the assessment?

IASME’s terms give six months from purchase. After that the account is closed and the fee is not refunded.
