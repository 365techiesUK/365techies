Why our number can differ from what Google shows

Most sea-temperature sites publish satellite-derived estimates of open water, sometimes updated daily, sometimes modelled from long-term analysis — and nearshore water can differ from those estimates by several degrees, as those sites’ own disclaimers note. The number above is a physical instrument in the bay reporting a measurement, with the time it was taken. When the instrument is down, we say so rather than switching to a model.

Sources: the bay’s wave buoy, the Environment Agency tide gauge at Bournemouth Pier and bathing-water service, and Wessex Water’s storm-overflow monitors — details at the foot of this page.

Can you swim today?

Put the live temperature above against the scale open-water swimmers actually use. The Outdoor Swimming Society’s guide — anecdotal rather than scientific, as the OSS itself says — runs: 0–6°C “Baltic”, 6–11°C “Freezing”, 12–16°C “Fresh”, 17–20°C “summer swimming”, 21°C+ “Warm”.

Two harder numbers sit alongside that. The RNLI defines anything below 15°C as cold water, capable of seriously affecting your breathing and movement — and their advice if you get into trouble is Float to Live: lean back, spread your arms and legs, and let the first minute of cold-water shock pass before you try to swim. And British Triathlon’s 2026 competition rules make wetsuits mandatory below 15.9°C and stop open-water racing entirely below 11°C — a fair guide to where “bracing” ends and “equipment required” begins.

In practice: a Bournemouth August afternoon is usually the friendliest sea of the year, and mid-winter is for the acclimatised, in company, close to shore. If the cold is new to you, build up over weeks of short swims from late spring — and swim between the red-and-yellow flags when lifeguards are on.

Bournemouth’s odd tide, live from the pier

The tide curve above often shows something tide tables flatten out: a long stand around high water, sometimes a visible double hump. It is not a faulty gauge — it is the English Channel’s geometry. The Channel behaves as a standing-wave system with a node near this coast, and in shallow water the tide’s harmonics distort the simple twice-a-day curve: Christchurch Harbour gets a true double high water on each tide, and Poole shows double highs at springs and a long stand at neaps (Humphreys, Salinity and Tides in Poole Harbour, Proceedings in Marine Science, 2005). Bournemouth sits between the two. Practical upshot: high water hangs around for hours — generous for swimmers, and the reason the beach can feel narrow all afternoon.

Water quality, beach by beach

The Environment Agency classifies seven bathing waters along this seafront — Alum Chine, Durley Chine, Bournemouth Pier, Boscombe Pier, Manor Steps, Fisherman’s Walk and Southbourne — and samples each through the May–September season. Swim with people who do this every week

Two long-running local groups, both active this year:

Durley Sea Swims — open-water and marathon-swim training from Durley Chine beach (in front of the lifeguard hut), weekend mornings May to September, suggested donation £3 a swim. Founded 2013 to support that year’s English Channel soloists, and still the serious-distance crowd. In the summer holidays they also run Friday-evening swims timed so you finish in the water as the Friday fireworks go up.

Bournemouth Sea Dippers — daily morning dips at Boscombe, six years running, and genuinely welcoming to newcomers. Cold-water dipping rather than distance swimming: short, sociable, year-round.

When the lifeguards are on

RNLI lifeguard patrols for the 2026 season (daily, 10am–6pm): Bournemouth East, Bournemouth West, Boscombe East and Sandbanks from 28 March to 27 September; Durley Chine, Alum Chine, Boscombe West, Fisherman’s Walk, Southbourne and Branksome from 23 May to 6 September; East Cliff and Manor Steps from 18 July to 6 September. Swim between the red-and-yellow flags. 2026 is the 25th year of RNLI lifeguards on these beaches — they began here as a 2001 pilot.

Dogs, barbecues and the rules

The same council rules cover all seven beaches. Dogs: not allowed on any of these beaches from 1 May to 30 September; welcome from 1 October to 30 April, on a lead at all times on promenades, piers, zig zags and footpaths; working guide dogs exempt year-round. Three adjacent stretches stay dog-friendly all year: the western side of Alum Chine towards Branksome Chine, Middle Chine to Durley Chine, and Fisherman’s Walk to Southbourne. Barbecues: disposables only, 6pm to 10:30pm — never gas barbecues, and never open fires, fire pits or fire bowls anywhere on the beaches, promenades or cliffs (fines can reach £1,000). Free electric barbecues run 10am–10pm from early summer to October: the largest bank sits just east of Boscombe Pier (10 standard, 5 accessible), with more at Fisherman’s Walk and Portman Ravine. Put disposables out with water and leave them next to a promenade bin — never buried, never binned hot.

Rules: BCP Council’s dog rules and barbecue rules; lifeguard dates: the RNLI’s 2026 season announcement and per-beach listings — checked 6 August 2026. Rules change; the council’s pages are definitive.

// GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

What is the sea temperature at Bournemouth today?

The live panel at the top of this page shows the current measured reading from the bay’s wave buoy, with the time it was taken. For context, the long-term August average is 18.4°C and February’s is 6.8°C (Cefas station 23, 1971–2000).

Is the sea at Bournemouth clean enough to swim in?

The Environment Agency’s latest classification rates all seven Bournemouth bathing waters Excellent — its top grade. In season it also issues a daily pollution-risk forecast, shown live above, and this page watches Wessex Water’s storm-overflow monitors around the clock. After heavy rain, quality can dip temporarily at some beaches — the honest move is to give it a day.

How warm does the sea get in Bournemouth?

The long-term August average is 18.4°C — the year’s peak — and warm spells push individual readings past 21°C, which is “Warm” on the Outdoor Swimming Society’s scale. The sea lags the air by about two months, so early summer is much colder than it looks.

Do I need a wetsuit to swim at Bournemouth?

Depends on the month and on you. British Triathlon’s 2026 rules make wetsuits mandatory in competition below 15.9°C — Bournemouth’s sea is typically below that from November to mid-June. The RNLI defines anything under 15°C as cold water. In high summer most swimmers go without; the rest of the year, a wetsuit is the sensible default unless you are acclimatised.

Where does this page’s data actually come from?

Physical instruments: the bay’s wave buoy (temperature and waves), the Environment Agency’s tide gauge mounted on Bournemouth Pier, the EA’s bathing-water sampling and daily risk forecasts, and Wessex Water’s storm-overflow monitors. Every reading is shown with the time it was measured — and when a feed is down, the page says so instead of guessing.
