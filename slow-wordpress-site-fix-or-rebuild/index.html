Slow WordPress site: when to fix it — and when to rebuild

Most slow WordPress sites can be sped up cheaply, and some genuinely should be rebuilt. The trouble is that almost everyone offering advice sells hosting, caching plugins or maintenance plans. Start here

The 20-minute triage

Before you spend a penny — on a plugin, a retainer or a rebuild quote — run five checks. They take about 20 minutes and they tell you which kind of slow you have.

  1. Run a speed test. Put your address into our free website speed test and look at the mobile result, because that is how most of your visitors arrive. Note the score down.
  2. Read the TTFB (time to first byte). This is how long your server takes to start responding, before your design even matters. Google’s own guidance on web.dev calls 0.8 seconds or less good and anything over 1.8 seconds poor. A high TTFB points at your hosting or a bloated database — no image tweak will rescue it.
  3. Count your active plugins. Log in to WordPress and count them honestly. Every one adds code, and struggling small-business sites routinely carry 30 or more, half of them forgotten.
  4. Check your hosting tier. If you are on the cheapest shared plan a host offers, your site shares a server with hundreds of strangers. That is often the whole story.
  5. Weigh your images. Photos uploaded straight from a phone can be 4–8 MB each. A handful of those on the homepage will sink any theme.

Now read the results together. Bad score but healthy TTFB and huge images? That is a cheap fix — start with the next section. Poor TTFB on decent hosting with a modest plugin list? The problem is structural, and the later sections matter more.

Try these first

Fixes that genuinely work and cost little or nothing

These are the fixes we actually apply, in the order that usually pays off. None of them requires buying anything from us.

  • Compress and resize images. The single biggest win on most small-business sites. Resize photos to the size they display at and compress them before upload; add an optimisation plugin to sweep the existing library.
  • Prune your plugins. Deactivate anything you do not recognise or use — then delete it, because deactivated plugins still pose a security risk. One good plugin often replaces three overlapping ones.
  • Set up caching properly. A caching plugin serves ready-made pages instead of rebuilding each one from the database. If you already have one and the site is still slow, the cache may not actually be firing — test a page as a logged-out visitor.
  • Update your PHP version. Hosts let old sites idle on outdated PHP for years. Moving to a current version in your hosting control panel is free and often noticeably faster — take a backup first.
  • Clean the database. Years of post revisions, spam comments and orphaned tables slow every query. A tidy-up plugin handles this safely.
  • Move up one hosting tier. If TTFB is your problem, this beats every plugin on this list.

Do these before anyone quotes you for anything. If your site is quick afterwards — excellent, you are done, and we are genuinely pleased for you. If it is still slow, keep reading.

The pattern to watch

The WordPress tax: paying forever to keep a slow thing slow

Here is the pattern we see again and again when a business brings us a tired WordPress site. There is a monthly maintenance fee. There are annual renewals for a premium theme, a page builder, a security plugin, a backup plugin. Every so often something breaks after an update and there is an invoice for putting it right. And after all of that… the site is still slow.

That happens because the weight is structural. A heavyweight theme, a page builder layered on top, a stack of plugins and a database round-trip for every visit add up to a site that no caching plugin can fully rescue — especially on shared hosting. You are not paying to make the site fast; you are paying to stop the complexity falling over.

At that point the question quietly changes. It stops being “how do I speed this up?” and becomes “why am I maintaining complexity I never use?” If your site is really ten pages that change twice a year, a great deal of what you are paying to patch, update and monitor exists only to support an editing system you rarely open.

If the slowness arrived suddenly rather than gradually — strange pages, spam links, a warning in Google — that is a different problem entirely: start with our website hacked checklist. And if you cannot even get into your own site to try the fixes above, see what to do when your web designer disappears or when access is being withheld.

The honest fork

Some slow sites should stay on WordPress

This is the part most guides skip, because the people writing them sell one answer or the other. Rebuilding away from WordPress is the wrong move if any of these describe you:

  • You edit content yourself, often. If you publish news, update prices or change pages weekly, WordPress’s editor is precisely what you are paying the overhead for. Keep it.
  • You run an online shop. WooCommerce sites need their database and plugins. Speed work here means better hosting and plugin discipline, not a platform change.
  • Members log in. Membership areas, courses and customer logins are dynamic by nature.
  • Multiple people write for the site. A multi-author blog with drafts, roles and scheduling is WordPress’s home turf.
  • A key plugin runs your business. Booking systems, directories and event calendars can be deeply wired in. Moving them is a project in itself — sometimes worth it, often not.

If that is you, the right path is fixing within WordPress: the cheap fixes above, a lighter theme, fewer and better plugins, and hosting that matches your traffic. We will happily say so, because we do not sell WordPress plugins, WordPress hosting or maintenance retainers — we have no stake in which answer you land on.

The rebuild case is the mirror image: a brochure site that changes a few times a year, edited by whoever built it anyway, carrying a plugin stack and monthly fees purely out of habit. For those sites, simpler is faster, safer and cheaper to live with.

// GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

Why is my WordPress site still slow even with caching?

Caching only helps with work the server repeats. If the slowness comes from multi-megabyte images, a heavyweight theme or page builder, too many plugins loading on every page, or a slow shared server (high TTFB), a cache cannot fix it. Test a page as a logged-out visitor too — many caches silently fail or exclude the pages that matter.

What is TTFB and what should mine be?

TTFB (time to first byte) is how long your server takes to begin responding, before any design loads. Google’s guidance on web.dev treats 0.8 seconds or less as good and over 1.8 seconds as poor. A high TTFB points at hosting or database problems rather than your theme or images.

How many WordPress plugins is too many?

There is no magic number — one badly written plugin can outweigh ten good ones. But if you count 30-plus and cannot say what half of them do, that is a red flag. Deactivate and delete anything unused; deactivated plugins still sit on your server as a security risk.

Is it worth fixing a slow WordPress site, or should I just rebuild?

Fix it if you edit content often, run a shop or memberships, or the triage points at cheap causes like oversized images and plugin bloat. Consider a rebuild if the site is a rarely edited brochure site, the slowness is structural, and you are paying monthly to keep it standing still. The 20-minute triage on this page tells you which side you are on.

Will rebuilding my website hurt my Google rankings?

Not if the migration is done properly — keeping your addresses where possible and putting a full 301-redirect map in place where they change, so Google follows every old page to its new home. That process is exactly what our website rebuild service covers, and it is how we moved our own site without losing rankings.

Do I have to leave WordPress to get a fast site?

No. Plenty of WordPress sites are quick — good hosting, a light theme and plugin discipline get most sites a long way. Leaving WordPress makes sense mainly when you are paying for editing and plugin complexity you never actually use.

My WordPress site keeps breaking as well as being slow. Is that connected?

Usually, yes — both come from the same place: a large stack of plugins and themes that all update on their own schedules and occasionally conflict. The bigger the stack, the more often something snaps. Pruning it helps both problems; if the site broke suddenly alongside strange content or warnings, check our website hacked checklist instead.

My site went slow suddenly rather than gradually. What does that mean?

Sudden slowness has a specific cause: a bad update, a hosting problem, or a compromise — hacked sites often slow down because they are quietly serving spam pages or malware. Check with your host first, and if you see unfamiliar pages, links or a browser warning, follow our website hacked checklist straight away.
