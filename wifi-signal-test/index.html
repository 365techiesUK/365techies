      // WHAT ACTUALLY HELPS

      The honest fixes, in order of what usually works

      Move the router, not the money

      The single most effective free fix. Routers belong out in the open, high up and central — not in a cupboard, behind a TV, on the floor or next to the microwave.

      Use the 5GHz network

      Most routers broadcast two networks. 5GHz is much faster but travels less far; 2.4GHz reaches further but is slower and more congested. Near the router, pick 5GHz.

      Mesh beats a booster

      If the problem is range (fine near the router, poor in the back bedroom), a mesh system genuinely fixes it. Cheap plug-in “boosters” often just repeat a weak signal — slower, not better.

      A new router won’t fix range

      If your speed is fine by the router and dies at the far end of the house, a faster router changes nothing. Be wary of anyone selling you one to fix that.

      WiFi 6 helps crowds, not distance

      WiFi 6/7 mainly helps when lots of devices compete at once. If you have a black-spot, it’s the wrong purchase — sort coverage first.

      If the line is slow, WiFi can’t save it

      If speed is low even standing next to the router, the problem is the broadband line or package, not the WiFi. That’s a different fix — and worth knowing before you spend.

      // READING THE NUMBERS

      What your reading actually means

      Signal strength is measured in dBm, and it is a negative number — closer to zero is better. Engineers design to a target of −67 dBm at the edge of coverage, so that is a sensible bar for any room you actually work or watch television in.

      • −30 to −55 dBm — excellent. You are close to the router with little in the way.
      • −55 to −67 dBm — fine for video calls, streaming and everything normal.
      • −67 to −75 dBm — marginal. It will connect, and it will disappoint.
      • Worse than −75 dBm — effectively unusable. This is the “full bars, nothing loads” zone.

      How to run this properly

      Test in the same spot in the same minute, and run a wired test first — that is your ceiling, and nothing wireless beats it. Record three numbers each time: download, upload and ping. On Windows, netsh wlan show interfaces tells you the band, channel and signal; on a Mac, hold Option and click the WiFi icon. iPhones do not expose signal strength, so use an Android handset if you are walking a survey.

      The test that matters more than speed

      Start a large download and watch your ping while it runs. If an idle ping of around 15 ms climbs to 250 ms under load, that is bufferbloat — and it is why calls break up the moment somebody else in the house uses the internet. It is invisible to a normal speed test, and it explains more complaints than any shortage of megabits.

      // GOOD QUESTIONS

      Frequently asked

      Can this show my WiFi name or whether I have WiFi 6?

      Honestly, no — and neither can any other website. Browsers deliberately don’t let a web page see your network name, the WiFi standard (WiFi 5/6/7), the band or the channel, because your network name can identify where you live. Only an app installed on the device can read those. What this tool measures instead is arguably more useful: whether the internet actually works well where you’re standing.

      So what is it actually measuring?

      Real, live network behaviour from your device: latency (how long a tiny request takes to reach our server and come back), jitter (how much that wobbles — the real giveaway for a weak or congested signal) and dropouts (requests that fail). We combine those into a 0–100 score. Nothing is guessed or made up.

      Why not just use the bars on my phone?

      The bars only tell you the strength of the radio signal — not whether the connection is usable. You can have full bars and still have unusable internet (congestion, interference, a struggling router, or a slow line). This measures what actually matters: can you really use the internet from this spot.

      Does it work on mobile data too?

      Yes. It measures the connection your device is actually using, so it works the same on 4G/5G — handy for finding the best spot for a signal in a rural house, a caravan or a workshop.

      Is it free, and do you keep my data?

      Completely free, no sign-up. Your whole survey — rooms, readings and the broadband details you type — is saved in your own browser only, and we never receive any of it unless you press the send button, which asks for your consent first. Our latency beacon stores nothing about you; it just replies with a single character (like any web request, our host keeps standard server logs). Two parts do involve a third party, and we’d rather say so: the provider/area lookup and the speed test run against Cloudflare’s network, so they see your IP address.

      Will my survey still be here if I come back later?

      Yes — it saves itself on this device as you go, and when you return you’ll get a “welcome back” with the option to carry on where you stopped. Two honest limits: it lives in this browser, so clearing your browsing data deletes it, and it can’t follow you to another device by itself. The Export button gives you a file you can keep or Import on any other device. In private browsing it may not survive closing the window at all — the tool will warn you.

      It says my signal is poor — what now?

      Scroll to the advice section: it uses your real readings to suggest what actually helps, and it will tell you honestly when something won’t help (a new router often doesn’t fix a range problem, for example). If you’d rather someone just sorted it, we cover Bournemouth, Poole and Dorset — WiFi support.
