/01 — START HERE

Years of email look gone? They almost certainly aren't

You've opened the new Outlook and years of email have apparently vanished — the inbox only goes back a few weeks or months, and folders that held a decade of correspondence look nearly empty. It's a horrible moment, so let's deal with the fear first: in almost every case, nothing has been deleted. Your old mail is still sitting in your mailbox on the server.

The new Outlook works differently from the Outlook many of us grew up with. Rather than downloading and keeping everything on your computer, it reads your mailbox from the server and keeps a recent slice of it locally for speed. When that slice is all you can see, it feels like data loss — but it's a display and sync issue, not a deletion.

On this page we'll show you how to prove your mail exists in about two minutes, where the setting that limits your view lives, why search can make things look worse than they are, and the one genuine exception — old archive files — where the right answer is a proper migration.

/02 — QUICK PROOF

Two minutes to prove your mail still exists

Before touching any settings, get your evidence. Open a web browser and sign in to your email provider's webmail — for Microsoft 365 and Outlook.com addresses that means signing in at office.com or outlook.com; other providers (BT, Gmail, your own domain's host) have their own webmail pages. Webmail shows you the mailbox as it actually exists on the server, with no app in the way.

If your old email is all there in webmail — and it usually is — you can relax. Nothing is lost, and the rest of this page is about persuading the app on your computer to show what the server already holds.

If the old mail isn't in webmail either, don't panic yet: this usually means it never lived on the server in the first place. Years of email from an older setup — particularly POP accounts and Classic Outlook — often sit in a local archive file on the computer instead. Skip to the last section below, because that's a different job with a happy ending of its own.

/03 — THE SETTING

The sync window: why the app only shows a recent slice

Mail apps limit how much they keep on the device to save space and stay fast. Somewhere in the new Outlook's settings — and in Classic Outlook's account settings — there's a control that decides how far back that local slice reaches. Look for wording along the lines of "download email for", "days of email to save" or "sync email from"; Microsoft moves the exact names and locations around between updates, which is why we're describing the setting rather than promising a click-path that may be wrong by the time you read this.

Set it to the longest window available — ideally "all" — then be patient. A large mailbox can take a while to come down, especially on a slower connection, and folders may fill in gradually rather than all at once.

Two honest notes. First, even with a short window the app should fetch older mail from the server when you scroll or search — but this fails often enough that lengthening the setting is still worth doing. Second, if the app is signed in but simply refuses to sync, signing out and back in — or removing and re-adding the account — resets a stuck sync more often than it should need to.

/04 — SEARCH QUIRK

The mail is there — but search says it isn't

A common variation: the old messages exist, and you can even reach them by scrolling, but typing a name or subject into search returns nothing older than a few weeks. That's a search problem, not a missing-mail problem, and it deserves its own page — see Outlook search not finding old emails for the full treatment.

The quick cross-checks: browse the folder sorted by date instead of searching, and try the same search in webmail. Webmail searches the server's full index, so if it finds the message and the app doesn't, you've confirmed the mail is safe and the fault is purely in the app's search.

If you're hitting several of these annoyances at once — missing mail, broken search, calendar oddities — our Outlook problems hub gathers the common new-Outlook complaints in one place, because they often share a cause.

/05 — HONEST ADVICE

When it's not worth fighting — and what we'd do

First, the free outcome: if webmail shows everything and lengthening the sync window brings it back into the app, you're done. Don't pay anyone — including us — for that.

Where we'd stop fighting: if your old email lives in PST archive files built up over years of Classic Outlook or an old POP account, the new Outlook is simply the wrong tool. Its support for opening old archives has been arriving slowly and unevenly, and we wouldn't gamble a decade of correspondence on it. Going back to Classic Outlook is free, legitimate, and opens those archives natively — and Microsoft has committed to supporting it for years to come.

The durable fix is a one-off migration: getting the archive off a single ageing hard drive and into a server-side mailbox or a properly backed-up store, so every app and phone can see it and one disk failure can't take it away. That's careful, slightly tedious work with real pitfalls — mailbox size limits, duplicates, corrupt archive files — and it's a job we do regularly as part of our email support. // GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

Has the new Outlook deleted my old emails?

Almost certainly not. The new Outlook reads your mailbox from the server rather than storing everything on your computer, and it keeps only a recent window of messages locally — so older mail can drop out of view without going anywhere. The fastest proof is signing in to your provider's webmail in a browser, where you see the mailbox exactly as it exists on the server. The one genuine exception is mail that only ever lived in a local archive file (a PST) on an older setup — that was never on the server, so a server-first app can't show it, but it still exists on the computer's drive.

Why does the new Outlook only show my recent emails and not old ones?

Because it's designed to keep a recent slice of your mailbox on the device and fetch the rest from the server on demand. If a sync or offline setting is set to a short window, the mailbox is very large, or the sync has quietly stuck, older messages fail to appear. There's also a separate case: if years of your email live in PST archive files from Classic Outlook or an old POP account, the new app can't simply open those, so everything before the switch looks missing. Checking webmail tells you which situation you're in. Note that classic Outlook needs a Microsoft 365 subscription or an Office licence that includes it — if new Outlook is all you have ever had, there may be nothing to switch back to.

How do I get the new Outlook to show all my old emails?

Find the sync or offline setting — the wording is along the lines of 'download email for' or 'days of email to save' — and set it to the longest option available (currently up to 180 days in new Outlook — only classic Outlook has an “All” setting) available, ideally all. Exact menu names move between versions, so we describe the setting rather than promise a click-path. Then give a large mailbox time to download; folders can fill in gradually. If the mail shows in webmail but still won't appear in the app, signing out and back in, or removing and re-adding the account, often resets a stuck sync. If none of that works, it's worth a professional look.

Where are my old emails if the new Outlook can't see them?

One of two places. Most commonly they're still in your mailbox on the server, which you can confirm by signing in to webmail in a browser — the app just isn't showing them. Otherwise they're in a local archive file (a PST) created by Classic Outlook, which is very common with older POP accounts that downloaded mail to the computer and removed it from the server. Those files sit on the computer's drive or an old backup, and the new Outlook can't reliably open them — so the mail looks gone when it's actually just in a format the new app doesn't read.

Should I go back to Classic Outlook to see my old emails?

If your old mail lives in PST archive files, yes — that's the pragmatic move. Classic Outlook opens those archives natively, switching back is free, and Microsoft has committed to supporting it for years to come, so it isn't a dead end. Longer term, the resilient answer is migrating that archive into a server-side mailbox or a properly backed-up store, so any app or phone can see it and a single hard-drive failure can't take a decade of email with it. That's a one-off job worth doing properly rather than fighting the new app indefinitely.
