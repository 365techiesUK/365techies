// TWO-BUILDING INTERNET

How to share one internet connection between two buildings

Got one broadband line and a second building that needs it—a workshop, annexe, barn or office? Here’s the honest ladder of ways to share your connection, from a buried cable to a point-to-point wireless bridge, and how we install them across Dorset. Rated 4.9 on Google.

/01 — THE SHORT ANSWER

The quickest way to share one connection across two buildings

To share one broadband connection between two buildings, you carry the signal from your main router into the second building using one of four methods—a buried Ethernet cable, powerline adapters, a mesh Wi-Fi system, or a point-to-point wireless bridge. For anything past a few metres, or where the two buildings sit on different electricity supplies, a wired Ethernet run or a wireless bridge is almost always the dependable answer.

Everything else is a trade-off between distance, whether you can dig a trench, and how much reliability you need. Here is the honest ladder, from cheapest and simplest to the option that copes with real distance:

  • Short gap, easy to dig: run outdoor-grade Ethernet and add a Wi-Fi access point at the far end.
  • Same electrical supply, short hop: powerline adapters may work—but often disappoint between buildings.
  • Close outbuilding, thin walls: a mesh node can reach a garden room, but struggles across distance or thick stone.
  • Distance, no trench, or line of sight across a gap: a point-to-point wireless bridge—our speciality.

We fit all of these across Bournemouth, Poole, Christchurch and the wider Dorset countryside, and we’ll tell you honestly which one suits your site before you spend a penny. If you’d like a hand choosing, a quick Wi-Fi installation chat is free.

/02 — OPTION 1: A WIRED RUN

A buried Ethernet cable: the gold standard

If you can get a cable between the buildings, a wired run is the gold standard—fastest, most stable, and immune to weather and interference. Use external-grade or direct-burial Ethernet cable (Cat5e or Cat6), ideally pulled through conduit so it is protected and replaceable, and route it at a sensible depth away from mains cables.

The one rule to remember: a single copper Ethernet run tops out at 100 metres (about 328 feet). Past that, speeds fall away and you start losing packets. If your buildings are further apart, you have two clean fixes—drop a small network switch in halfway to start a fresh 100-metre segment, or step up to fibre-optic cable, which happily runs far further.

Outdoors you also want surge protection at each end, because a long copper cable strung between two buildings can pick up electrical spikes in a storm. At the far end, plug the cable into a switch or a Wi-Fi access point—see our home network setup guide—and the second building has full-speed internet as if it were in the same room.

The catch is the digging. If a trench across a yard, driveway or field is impractical, that is exactly where a wireless bridge earns its keep.

/03 — OPTION 2: POWERLINE

Powerline adapters: simple, but often a let-down between buildings

Powerline (or ‘homeplug’) adapters send your network signal through the existing electrical wiring—plug one in near the router, another in the second building, and in theory the internet travels down the mains. When it works it’s wonderfully simple. Between two separate buildings, though, it often doesn’t.

The reason is electrical, not digital. Powerline needs a clean, continuous wiring path between the two adapters. If your outbuilding has its own consumer unit (fuse box), its own meter, or a separate supply from the road, the signal has to cross that boundary—and it usually can’t. Even a shared supply on different circuits can knock the speed down badly.

So powerline is worth a try only when the second building is fed from the same consumer unit as the house, on a short cable run—think an attached garage or a garden office wired off the main board. It’s cheap enough to test and return if it fails.

If the buildings are on different supplies, don’t waste money on it—skip straight to a proper cable or a wireless bridge. We can check your setup and tell you in a couple of minutes whether powerline stands any chance on your property.

/04 — OPTION 3: MESH WI-FI

Mesh Wi-Fi: great last hop, poor way to cross the gap

A mesh Wi-Fi system uses several nodes that talk to each other to blanket an area in signal, and it’s brilliant inside a house. Push it outdoors between buildings and its limits show quickly. Wi-Fi weakens fast with distance, and the things between your buildings—cavity walls, stone, a metal shed roof, even wet foliage—soak up or bounce the signal away.

Metal and water are the worst offenders: a steel-clad workshop or a barn with a tin roof can act almost like a shield. A mesh node sitting in a window facing a nearby garden room might just reach, but you’ll often get a weak, dropping link that frustrates everyone who relies on it.

Where mesh does shine is after you’ve carried a solid connection into the second building by cable or bridge—then a node there gives you lovely Wi-Fi throughout that space. In other words, mesh is a great last hop, not a great way to cross the gap. For a nearby annexe our granny-annexe Wi-Fi guide walks through exactly when a mesh node is enough and when it isn’t.

/05 — OPTION 4: WIRELESS BRIDGE

Point-to-point wireless bridge: our specialism

When there’s distance to cover and digging a trench isn’t practical, a point-to-point wireless bridge is usually the answer—and it’s our specialism. Two small weatherproof units are mounted outdoors, one on each building, and aimed directly at each other. Together they form a focused, high-speed radio link that behaves like an invisible Ethernet cable strung between the two roofs.

One unit connects to your main router with a short cable; the other feeds a switch or access point in the second building. They’re typically powered over the network cable (PoE), so you only need to run one lead to each. The single firm requirement is a clear line of sight—the two units need to ‘see’ each other, with trees, walls and outbuildings out of the path.

Off-the-shelf bridge kits comfortably cover distances up to around a kilometre, and with higher-gain directional antennas and clear line of sight you can go much further. UK radio rules cap the power these devices may transmit, which is one reason a proper install and correct aiming matters—done well, a bridge is fast, stable and barely noticeable. This is a core part of our rural and farm Wi-Fi work.

/06 — WHICH FITS YOUR SITE

Which option is right for your two buildings?

Choosing between the four comes down to three questions: how far apart are the buildings, can you dig, and are they on the same electricity supply?

  • Under 100m and you can trench: a buried Ethernet run wins on speed and reliability every time.
  • Attached or same fuse box, short hop: powerline is worth a cheap test, with a cable as backup.
  • Very close, thin walls, light use: a mesh node may reach—fine for a bit of browsing, not for a busy office.
  • Real distance, a trench you can’t or won’t dig, or clear line of sight across a gap: a point-to-point wireless bridge.

Real sites are rarely tidy, so we often combine them—a wireless bridge to cross a paddock, then a wired access point or mesh node inside the far building to spread the signal around. A workshop that needs card payments and stock lookups, a holiday let above a garage, a granny annexe, a barn office—each has a best answer, and it’s not always the most expensive one.

If your broadband itself is the weak link, it’s worth sorting that first; our business broadband and connectivity page covers getting a solid line into the main building before you share it.

/07 — HOW WE INSTALL IT

How we install building-to-building links across Dorset

This is genuinely one of the things we love doing. As a family-run Dorset firm going since 1995, we’ve run links across yards, gardens, farmyards and fields all over Bournemouth, Poole, Christchurch and rural Dorset—so we can look at your two buildings, check the line of sight, and recommend the option that will still be working in five years, not just on the day.

Back in 2015 we installed a point-to-point wireless link across the River Avon for the Environment Agency, carrying connectivity for a fish counter—we provided the link, not the counter. If we can beam a reliable connection across a river, a driveway or a paddock is well within reach.

A typical job is a site visit to confirm line of sight and power, a tidy install of the units and cabling, and testing at both ends so the second building gets a fast, stable connection. Most of our support is remote, but a building-to-building link is hands-on, on-site work, so we’ll always come out and quote it properly first.

// GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

What’s the best way to share one internet connection between two buildings?

For most UK properties it’s a straight choice between a buried Ethernet cable, if you can dig a trench under 100 metres, and a point-to-point wireless bridge, if you can’t dig or the buildings are further apart. Powerline and mesh Wi-Fi can work for short, easy hops but often disappoint between separate buildings. The right pick depends on distance, line of sight and your electrics.

How far apart can the two buildings be?

A single copper Ethernet run stretches to 100 metres before speeds drop; add a switch halfway or use fibre to go further. A point-to-point wireless bridge comfortably covers up to around a kilometre with clear line of sight, and much more with higher-gain antennas. Distance itself is rarely the blocker—obstacles in the path usually matter more.

Will powerline adapters work between two buildings?

Only if the second building runs off the same consumer unit (fuse box) as the first, on a short wiring path. If your outbuilding has its own fuse box, its own meter, or a separate supply from the road, powerline almost always fails or crawls. It’s cheap to test, but don’t count on it across separate buildings—have a cable or bridge as plan B.

Do I need line of sight for a wireless bridge?

Yes—a clear line of sight between the two units is the one firm requirement. They need to ‘see’ each other with no buildings, dense trees or walls in the way, and mounting them high on a wall or pole usually solves it. If a tree or barn sits in the path, we’ll look at re-siting the units or a different route on the day.

Can a wireless bridge match a wired connection?

Honestly, a good wired run is still the fastest and most rock-solid option, and if you can dig a trench under 100 metres we’ll often recommend it. A well-installed bridge comes very close and is more than enough for internet, card machines, cameras and cloud apps—but heavy rain, a knocked antenna or a new obstruction can affect a radio link in ways a buried cable never will. We’ll always tell you the trade-off.

Can you just come and do it for me?

Yes—that’s exactly what we do. We’ll visit your property across Bournemouth, Poole, Christchurch or Dorset, check the line of sight and power, recommend the right option, and handle the full install and testing at both ends. You don’t need to understand any of the kit—call 01202 775566 and we’ll take it from there.

Will it work for a workshop, annexe or barn office?

Almost always. A workshop that needs card payments, a holiday let above a garage, a granny annexe or a barn office are all common jobs for us. We usually carry a solid connection into the building by cable or bridge, then add a Wi-Fi access point or mesh node inside so the whole space is covered.

Is this the same as extending my Wi-Fi?

Not quite. Extending Wi-Fi tries to push your existing signal further, which fades fast through walls and across gardens. Sharing internet between buildings means carrying the connection itself—by cable or a focused wireless bridge—into the second building, then creating fresh, strong Wi-Fi there. That’s why the result is so much more reliable than a repeater in a window.

What if the second building has no broadband nearby at all?

If neither building has a usable line, we look at the connection first—sometimes Starlink or a fixed-wireless service into the main building, then a bridge or cable to the second. Rural sites are our bread and butter; our rural and farm Wi-Fi page covers the options when you’re off the beaten track.

One connection, two buildings—sorted.

Tell us about your two buildings and we’ll recommend the right option and quote it—free advice, no pressure, right across Bournemouth, Poole, Christchurch and Dorset.
