WiFi heatmap online, free, no download — the honest answer

You want to see where your WiFi is strong and weak without installing anything. Fair enough — but no website can genuinely do that, because browsers can't read signal strength. /01 — THE STRAIGHT ANSWER

No browser can make a true WiFi heatmap — here's why, and what to do instead

You typed something like "wifi heatmap online free no download" hoping a website could scan your house and paint a coloured map of where the signal is strong and weak. We'll be straight with you, because most pages ranking for this search aren't: no website can do that. A real heatmap plots radio signal strength — measured in dBm — at dozens of points on your floor plan, and web browsers are deliberately locked out of your WiFi radio. There is no browser feature that lets a page read signal strength, see nearby networks, or even know which access point you're connected to; it would be a privacy disaster if there were.

So any site promising a heatmap "online, no download" is either guessing from a speed test or drawing pretty pictures. But your search isn't a dead end. Below: what a genuine heatmap actually requires, the genuinely useful thing a browser can measure room by room — which for most homes is the better answer anyway — and our honest take on when to spend nothing at all, and when it's worth calling someone like us.

/02 — HOW REAL HEATMAPS WORK

A true heatmap needs installed software reading your WiFi card

A genuine WiFi heatmap works like this: you install an app on a laptop — NetSpot and Ekahau are the best-known names in this class — load or sketch your floor plan, then walk the building clicking your position as you go. The app reads the signal strength your WiFi adapter is actually receiving at each point, then fills in the gaps to produce those familiar red-to-green colour maps. The measurement is the whole product, and it can only come from software with direct access to the wireless hardware — exactly what a browser is forbidden from having.

Free options do exist, with caveats. Some heatmap apps offer a free tier for home use, though what's included changes over time, so check the current limits rather than taking an old article's word for it. On Android, free WiFi analyser apps can show live signal strength as you walk, which makes a rough-and-ready survey; Apple restricts this on iPhone, so an iPhone alone can't do the job. And if you just want one honest number with nothing new installed, Windows already has it: open a command prompt, type netsh wlan show interfaces, and look at the Signal line for your current connection.

/03 — WHAT A BROWSER CAN DO

What a browser can measure — often the more useful map

Here's the part most pages skip: signal strength was only ever a proxy for the thing you actually care about. Nobody's real question is "what dBm am I getting in the back bedroom?" It's "why do video calls break up in the back bedroom?" — and a browser can measure that directly. From a web page we can time how long data takes to travel (latency), how much that varies (jitter, the thing that wrecks calls), whether packets go missing, and how fast the connection really runs — separately, in every room you stand in.

Do that in each room and you've built a connection-quality map: not a coloured floor plan, but an honest picture of where your WiFi works and where it doesn't. Often it's more useful than a heatmap, because it catches problems signal strength misses — a room can show full bars and still stutter thanks to interference or congestion, which is common in UK brick and stone homes where the building itself shapes the signal. Our free WiFi signal test measures exactly these things from your browser — nothing to install, nothing to sign up for.

/04 — DIY IN TEN MINUTES

Build your own connection-quality map in about ten minutes

The DIY version takes about ten minutes with a laptop or phone and our free room-by-room WiFi test. Stand where you actually use the internet — sofa, desk, kitchen table, the bedroom where films buffer — run the test in each spot, and save a reading for every room as you go — our tool scores each room, star-rates it and builds a report you can download. At the end you have a room-by-room comparison rather than a vague sense that "upstairs is worse".

Then read it the way an engineer would:

  • Speed falls off a cliff in certain rooms — distance and walls; classic signal decay. Router placement or a mesh node is the likely fix.
  • Speed is fine but jitter or packet loss is high — interference or a congested band; moving the router won't help much, changing channel or band might.
  • One device struggles where others are fine — suspect the device, not the WiFi.

Settings screens move between routers and Windows versions, so we won't pretend to give you exact click-paths — but the logic holds everywhere: measure where you actually live, compare, and the weak spot names itself.

/05 — OUR HONEST ADVICE

When it's not worth fighting it — and what we'd actually do

Our honest advice, as people who fix this for a living: for a normal home, don't buy survey software, and think twice before spending an afternoon building a heatmap at all. Most domestic WiFi problems come down to a router in a terrible spot (cupboards and floor level are the usual suspects), one dead zone that a single mesh node cures, or old kit that was never going to cover a three-storey house. A ten-minute room-by-room test finds those. A heatmap finds the same thing, prettier and slower.

Where we would use proper survey kit — and do — is where the building or the stakes are bigger: offices with several access points, thick-walled or steel-framed buildings, sites where a dropped connection costs money, or anywhere about to spend properly on new networking and wanting it placed right first time. That's what our business WiFi installation and survey service is for — we measure on site with real tools and design around what the building actually does to the signal.

// GOOD QUESTIONS

Frequently asked

Can I make a WiFi heatmap online without downloading anything?

No — a real heatmap needs installed software that reads signal strength from your WiFi adapter, and web browsers are not allowed to do that. Any site offering an "online heatmap" is estimating from speed tests at best. What a browser genuinely can do is measure the quality of your connection — speed, delay, stability — from each room, which for most people answers the real question ("why is WiFi bad in that room?") just as well. Our free room-by-room test does exactly that, with nothing to install and nothing to sign up for.

Why can't a website measure my WiFi signal strength?

Because browsers deliberately have no access to your WiFi radio. There is no web feature that exposes signal strength, nearby network names, or which access point you're connected to — and for good reason: websites being able to scan the networks around you would be a serious privacy problem, since nearby network names can reveal where you are. This is a rule of how the web works, not a limitation of any one tool, so it applies equally to every site claiming to offer an online heatmap.

Is there a genuinely free WiFi heatmap app?

Yes — free heatmap apps exist for laptops, in the NetSpot class, though free tiers are limited and the limits change over time, so check before relying on one. You install the app, load or sketch a floor plan, and walk the house clicking your position while it reads signal strength. On Android, free WiFi analyser apps can show live signal as you walk; Apple restricts this on iPhone, so an iPhone alone can't do a proper survey. If that sounds like more effort than the problem deserves, it usually is — a room-by-room quality test is faster.

Do I actually need a heatmap to fix bad WiFi?

Usually not. A heatmap tells you where signal is weak, which you mostly already know — it's the room you searched from. What actually fixes WiFi is acting on that: moving the router somewhere sensible, adding a mesh node halfway to the dead zone, or shifting a stuttering device to a less congested band. A simple room-by-room test that measures real performance usually points at the same fix a heatmap would, in a tenth of the time. Heatmaps earn their keep in larger or trickier buildings — and that's professional survey territory.

When is a professional WiFi survey worth paying for?

When the building or the stakes outgrow DIY: business premises with multiple access points, thick brick or steel-framed buildings, warehouses, surgeries or offices where dropped connections cost money, or anywhere about to spend serious money on new kit that needs placing right first time. A professional survey uses proper equipment to map signal and interference on site. For a normal home, we'd honestly steer you away from paying for one — a free room-by-room test and a well-placed mesh node solve most houses. We cover Bournemouth, Poole and the surrounding area.
